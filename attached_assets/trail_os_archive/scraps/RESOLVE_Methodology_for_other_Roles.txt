The key pivot

“How do we analyze a problem so we can design and build the right Salesforce solution?”

“How do we understand, simplify, and prepare a process so automation becomes possible, useful, and responsible?”
That means the output is not necessarily a Flow, app, dashboard, or Salesforce build.
The output could be:
a clarified business process
a decision tree
an automation readiness checklist
a cleaned-up handoff process
a list of required data fields
a process map
a risk and exception log
a “do not automate yet” recommendation
a future automation brief for a Salesforce admin, consultant, or AI tool
Tiny but important distinction: this version helps people become automation thinkers before automation builders.

The big philosophical shift
The existing R.E.S.O.L.V.E. materials already say that clarity comes before configuration and that digital noise is often a structural failure rather than just a software limitation.
For automation readiness, I would make that the headline principle:
Automation does not fix confusion. It accelerates whatever process already exists.
So before someone automates, they need to ask:
Is the process consistent enough to automate?
Are the decision rules clear?
Is the data reliable?
Do people agree on what “done” means?
Are exceptions documented?
Is this a high-value use of automation, or are we automating clutter with a tiny robot broom?
That last one is a very real business risk. Tiny robot broom syndrome is rampant.
How each phase could work
R: Recognize the Automation Opportunity
This phase is about spotting where automation might help, without assuming automation is the answer.
The learner identifies:
where work is repetitive
where delays happen
where people duplicate effort
where handoffs break down
where data is copied between systems
where staff rely on memory, sticky notes, spreadsheets, or Slack archaeology
where clients, donors, staff, or partners experience friction
The key question:
What business pain keeps repeating, and what does it cost us in time, quality, money, trust, or momentum?
Deliverable:
Automation Opportunity Statement
Example:
Intake requests are coming in through email, forms, and Slack. Staff manually review, categorize, assign, and follow up. This causes missed requests, inconsistent response times, and limited visibility into workload.

E: Explore the Current-State Process
This phase slows everything down, in a good way.
The learner maps the real process, not the imaginary “official” process that lives in a dusty SOP goblin cave.
They document:
who starts the process
what triggers it
what information is collected
where the information goes
who touches it
what decisions are made
what tools are used
what exceptions occur
where people wait
where errors happen
what workarounds people use
The key question:
How does this work actually happen today, from first trigger to final outcome?
Deliverable:
Current-State Workflow Map
This phase should include interviews, observation, sample records, screenshots, and “walk me through the last real example” conversations.

S: Select the Best Automation Candidate
Not every process is ready for automation.
This phase helps learners choose the part of the workflow that is most valuable and realistic to prepare.
They evaluate:
frequency: does this happen often?
consistency: does it follow a repeatable pattern?
impact: would improvement matter?
risk: what happens if it goes wrong?
data readiness: is the required information available?
ownership: does someone own the process?
exception volume: are there too many edge cases?
user readiness: will people actually use the improved process?
The key question:
Which part of this process is stable, valuable, and clear enough to prepare for automation?
Deliverable:
Automation Candidate Scorecard
This is where people learn that the best first automation is often not the flashiest one. It is the one with enough structure to survive contact with Monday morning.

O: Outline the Automation-Ready Process
This is the heart of the new version.
The learner outlines what needs to be true before anything gets built.
They define:
trigger: what starts the process?
input: what data is required?
rules: what decisions must be made?
routing: who or what receives the work?
actions: what should happen automatically?
exceptions: when should a human intervene?
notifications: who needs to know what?
data updates: what records should change?
success outcome: what does “complete” mean?
audit trail: what needs to be tracked?
The key question:
What would a clean, automation-ready version of this process look like?
Deliverable:
Automation Readiness Brief
This could become a handoff artifact for Salesforce, Zapier, Make, Slack, Jira, Monday.com, or even an AI-supported workflow.
The point is not tool selection yet. The point is business clarity.

L: Launch the Manual Prototype or Automation Brief
For the business-thinking version, Launch does not have to mean “deploy the automation.”
It could mean:
test the future-state process manually
launch a standardized intake form
pilot a new checklist
test routing rules with a small group
validate the automation brief with stakeholders
confirm that the proposed rules work in real life
hand off the brief to a builder
The key question:
Can this improved process work manually before we ask technology to repeat it at scale?
Deliverable:
Pilot Workflow or Builder-Ready Handoff
This is where I would teach the principle:
If the process cannot work manually, automation will not magically bless it with order.
Automation is not a wizard. It is a very literal intern with lightning speed.

V: Verify Business Readiness and Value
Here, learners measure whether the process is truly clearer and more automation-ready.
They look at:
time saved
fewer missed steps
fewer duplicate entries
clearer ownership
cleaner data
better response times
fewer questions from users
easier reporting
reduced manual follow-up
user confidence
The key question:
Did this process become clearer, more consistent, and more measurable?
Deliverable:
Readiness and Value Review
This is also where learners decide whether the next step should be:
automate now
refine the process first
collect better data
train users
reduce exceptions
redesign the workflow
abandon automation because the value is not there
That last one matters. A mature automation thinker can say, “Not yet.”

E: Evolve the Process and Automation Plan
The current methodology already treats Evolve as a loop back into Recognize, where feedback reveals the next problem or opportunity.
In this business automation version, Evolve becomes a governance habit.
The learner asks:
What changed in the business?
What new exceptions appeared?
What data quality issues surfaced?
Are users bypassing the process?
Has volume increased?
Are the rules still accurate?
Should this now become a technical automation?
Should the automation be expanded, simplified, or retired?
The key question:
What did we learn, and what needs to change before we scale?
Deliverable:
Process Evolution Log
This makes automation feel less like a one-time build and more like a living business capability.

1. Purpose of This Approach
Transition Trails is adapting the R.E.S.O.L.V.E. methodology into a Business Analyst learning model that helps learners think through business problems before building or automating technology solutions.
The first major use case will be a Salesforce Business Analyst learning experience built around the Horizon Community Hub, or HCH, capstone project. This approach will support two learning pathways:
Guided Trail BA LensA lighter version embedded into the Guided Trail experience so learners begin practicing BA thinking while working through the HCH capstone.
Trail of Mastery BA Product LabA deeper, role-based project lab where learners step into the Business Analyst role and use the HCH capstone to prepare for Salesforce Business Analyst certification and BA-style project work.
The goal is not to turn every learner into a technical builder. The goal is to teach learners how to analyze business needs, clarify requirements, prepare automation-ready process thinking, support validation, and connect Salesforce work to measurable business value.

2. Strategic Framing
Core Idea
R.E.S.O.L.V.E. becomes a structured thinking model for Salesforce Business Analysts.
Instead of asking only:
How do we build the right Salesforce solution?
The BA version asks:
How do we understand the business problem, clarify the process, define the requirements, validate the solution, and prepare the organization for successful automation or improvement?
This framing is especially valuable because many organizations, especially nonprofits, often start with a tool request before the process is clear.
Examples:
“We need Salesforce to automate intake.”
“We need Zapier to connect everything.”
“We need AI to summarize donor notes.”
“We need a dashboard.”
The BA learner is trained to pause and ask:
What problem are we actually solving?
Who is affected?
What happens today?
What needs to change?
What should be automated, simplified, clarified, or left manual?
How will we know the solution worked?
Guiding Principle
Before you automate the work, understand the work.
Supporting Principle
Automation does not fix confusion. It accelerates whatever process already exists.
The BA Product Lab gives learners a safe but realistic environment to practice this kind of thinking.

Example Requirement Expansion
High-Level Need
HCH must respond to urgent service requests within a 4-hour window.
Business Requirement
HCH needs a repeatable process to identify urgent intake requests, assign ownership, and monitor response deadlines.
Functional Requirements
The system must capture service request type.
The system must identify urgent request categories.
The system must create a case connected to the client record.
The system must assign urgent cases to the correct queue or staff member.
The system must track the response deadline.
The system must notify staff when urgent action is required.
The system must support reporting on urgent response performance.
Business Rules
If request type equals urgent housing or utility shut-off, response target is 4 hours.
If required intake fields are missing, the case should be flagged for review.
If no owner accepts the case within a defined period, escalation should occur.
Acceptance Criteria
Given a new urgent request is submitted, when required fields are complete, then a case is created with urgent priority.
Given an urgent case is assigned, when the case is created, then the response deadline is calculated.
Given an urgent case approaches the deadline, when no action has been logged, then the assigned staff member receives an escalation alert.
Given a manager views urgent cases, when they open the dashboard, then they can see open cases by deadline status.

13. Program Design Recommendations
Recommendation 1: Build the Trail of Mastery BA Product Lab First
The full Product Lab should be designed first because it creates the complete framework, artifact set, and certification alignment.
Once complete, lighter pieces can be pulled into Guided Trail as BA Lens prompts.
Recommendation 2: Keep Guided Trail Lightweight
The Guided Trail version should not become a second full BA course. It should introduce BA thinking through practical prompts, reflections, and Admin Decision Log enhancements.
Recommendation 3: Use HCH as a Shared Capstone Universe
Do not create a separate case study for BA learners yet. Use HCH as the shared project ecosystem so learners can experience the same scenario from different professional roles.
Recommendation 4: Make the BA Role Explicit
Every module should remind learners:
You are the Business Analyst. What do you need to understand, document, clarify, validate, or escalate?
Recommendation 5: Separate BA Work from Builder Work
The BA learner may understand Salesforce concepts, but the Product Lab should not require them to personally configure every component.
Use BA verbs:
Analyze
Clarify
Document
Validate
Trace
Recommend
Facilitate
Prepare
Review
Triage
Connect to business value
Avoid making the BA lab primarily about:
Configure
Build
Deploy
Debug
Administer
Recommendation 6: Tie Every Artifact to Certification and Career Readiness
Each artifact should answer:
What BA skill does this demonstrate?
How does this support Salesforce Business Analyst certification readiness?
How could the learner discuss this in an interview?
