Transition Trails · Explorer’s Trail
One membership, three audiences
Explorer’s Trail is the only program that is both the front door and the alumni home. That is its strength and its whole design problem — the curious, the practising, and the graduated want opposite things from the same subscription.
01 · The design problem
Three audiences with opposite needs
The site already names them: curious minds, active learners, working alumni. What it does not yet resolve is that each one measures value differently, and a membership that optimises for one loses the others. Naming what each is actually buying is the whole design.
Curious
Buying permission to start
They are deciding whether Salesforce is for them. What they need is a low-stakes first win inside two weeks, and an honest answer about what this career actually involves. What kills them is a wall of content.
Churn risk:
month two, having read a lot and built nothing.
Active
Buying a habit and a next step
Studying for a cert, or filling gaps their accidental-admin job created. They need something new every month at their level, and a visible route into Foundations or Guided Trail. This is the tier that converts.
Churn risk:
the month a mission is too easy, or too hard, twice in a row.
Alumni
Buying belonging and standing
They are employed. They do not need Trail Missions. They stay for the people, for staying current, and for a way to give back that is visible on a LinkedIn profile. Content will never hold them.
Churn risk:
nothing to do. Which is why the coach ladder matters more than any perk.
Coach’s Assistant, Associate Coach, Advanced TT Coach is on the page as a pathway, but it is really the answer to alumni retention — and simultaneously the supply line for the coaches Guided Trail and the apprenticeships consume. Treat it as the alumni tier’s core benefit rather than a career perk, and one asset solves retention, delivery capacity, and founder-independence at once.

03 · The monthly unit
A Trail Mission is the only thing that has to arrive on time
Four skill levels, one new mission each per month, self-contained and with no walkthrough. Everything else in the membership is optional — this is not. A month without a mission is a month a member cannot justify the charge, and the four-level commitment means four pieces of content monthly, forever.
Mission anatomy — already right in the blueprint
A brief framing the business context · the skill level · the task · no steps. A Trailhead module teaches what a feature is; a Trail Mission asks you to use it. That distinction is the product, and it is the same distinction Guided Trail makes with Option Cards versus walkthroughs.
Four levels a month is the real cost
Twelve months at four levels is forty-eight missions a year, and Expert-level content is the hardest to write and the least consumed. Two protections: let Expert be quarterly rather than monthly and say so plainly, and let alumni contribute missions — which is both content supply and the standing they are actually buying.
Self-selection into four levels is the mechanic most likely to fail quietly — a member who picks wrong twice leaves without telling you. Penny already recommends the next mission based on goals in the blueprint; make that the default entry rather than a feature, and give her the skill assessment result to work from. In Foundations Penny is the primary coach because the trail can be walked alone; the same is true here, more so.
04 · Earned access
Some memberships are earned, and that is the retention engine
Guided Trail completion earns membership; Guided Trail plus an apprenticeship earns it for life. Two paths, both earned by finishing something — with the trial concept dropped, "earned" keeps its meaning. A graduate never leaves the ecosystem and the alumni tier fills itself. Two things to make it work.
Activate it automatically, from the program record.
The blueprint already says access activates on completion confirmation. That means completion has to be a queryable state — which is exactly what the rubric produces. A graduate should never have to ask.
Decide what lifetime means when the price changes.
Lifetime at annual level is a promise made in 2026 against a product that will not look the same in 2030. Say what it covers — community, missions, dashboard — and what it does not, which is coaching hours and anything with a marginal cost.
04b · The Trail Dashboard
Four things, all of them live
Every element is either a number that moved or something to do this week. Missions completed is a vanity count and does not appear. The test for anything added later: could a member act on it today, or did it just make them feel busy?
Skills, with movement
From the skill assessment, by domain, showing the change since last time — not a total. The same instrument the other programs use, so a member who later enrols arrives with a baseline already recorded.
Goals the member set
What they are working toward, in their words, so Penny is coaching against something real rather than guessing. This is the single most important field on the dashboard — without it Penny can only recommend by level, and a level is not a goal.
This month’s Trail Mission
Front and centre on the first of the month, at their level, with how many others are working it. The monthly drop is the thing people should look forward to — treat it like a release, not a library addition.
Trail Quests available now
Short, Penny-set, and open to everyone — the same mechanic Foundations uses nightly, here as something to pick up. And if the member is a coach, their coaching queue appears alongside it rather than in a separate place.
A member who has told the system "I want to be hired as an admin within a year" gets different advice from one who wrote "I want to stop being scared of Flow Builder." Both are real, and neither can be inferred from a skill level. Capture the goal at signup, revisit it quarterly, and let Penny reference it by name — that is the difference between a dashboard that recommends content and one that feels like someone is paying attention.
05 · Open
Two settled, one open
1. Settled — the series is awareness, and a doorway.
Explorer’s Trail teaches the vocabulary, the seven phases, and why clarity precedes configuration. It is deliberately enticing rather than complete: someone who has not taken a program should finish it wanting the practitioner module. Guided Trail owns application against real work. Rename accordingly so the two never read as the same thing —
RESOLVE Foundations
here,
RESOLVE Practitioner
there.
2. Open — the coaching model needs rethinking, not capping.
Penny is the primary coach here and a TT coach is
aware
rather than on call. That changes the shape: instead of a 48-hour SLA on every tier, Penny handles the member continuously and surfaces the ones who need a human. A coach reviews what Penny escalates. The design question is what triggers an escalation — repeated failure on the same skill, a goal with no movement for a month, or a member who asks — and how a coach is notified without being on the hook for everyone.
