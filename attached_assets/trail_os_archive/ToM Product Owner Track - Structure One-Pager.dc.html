The Product Owner Role Track

A structure to pressure-test before anything long gets written. 12 weeks, squad-embedded, real nonprofit client work.

ToM–PO · role track inside ToM Prereq: Admin cert + currently employed Draft v0.1 · July 2026
01 · The bar

In a configuration platform, the how is a product decision

Whether something is a formula field, a rollup, a Flow, or Apex determines who can maintain it, what breaks at scale, and what the next change costs. Those are product properties. A PO who cannot reason about them makes commitments the platform will refuse to keep.

The graduation bar — three sentences
  1. You can build a rough version of it yourself in a sandbox — enough to know whether the estimate you are given is plausible.
  2. You can recognize a bad implementation before it ships, name the smell, and raise it in the room.
  3. You can defend a prioritization decision, including what you said no to and what it cost.
02 · Certification anchor

You are right to distrust PSPO. Anchor to Salesforce's own Business Analyst exam instead.

Your Assessment Framework has a non-negotiable: every question traces to a published exam guide. PSPO would satisfy the letter of that rule and fail the spirit — it tests ceremony vocabulary, not platform judgment. But Salesforce publishes an exam guide that covers almost exactly the PO craft you care about, and it is a Salesforce document: Salesforce Certified Business Analyst.

Craft anchorPrimary
Salesforce Certified Business Analyst — discovery, stakeholder collaboration, process mapping, requirements, user stories, user acceptance. Weighted roughly evenly across six domains; confirm the live weights against the current exam guide before we write Penny content.
Defensible to funders, published by Salesforce, and a credential a learner can actually sit at the end.
Platform-literacy anchorReweighted
Admin exam domains you already own — reused, but reweighted for a PO. Process Automation, Object Manager, Security & Access, and Data Management carry the weight. Sales & Marketing Applications carries almost none.
Zero new content to author. The question bank exists — only the gate thresholds change.
Teach the 2020 Scrum Guide accountabilities so a learner can hold their own in a room where those words are used, and let PSPO I be an optional self-study add-on the learner pays for if they want the résumé line. It is never a gate, and TT never markets it as the outcome. The outcome is the evidence.
Why this beats a TT-invented competency model: you keep the "we do not invent frameworks" principle intact, and a nonprofit board or a funder can look up both anchors themselves. Why it beats PSPO alone: nothing in PSPO tells you whether a Flow doing twelve things is a problem.
03 · Capstone

Three ways to shape it

The tension: real client work guarantees consequences but not coverage. A fixed capstone guarantees coverage but not consequences. You need both, so the question is only how they interleave.

Option A
Squad-embedded only
The PO owns a real DC client backlog from week 1. No synthetic work at all.
Cost: coverage is luck. A calm client means a PO who never had to say no.
Option B · recommended
Inherit Horizon, then ship for a client
Sprint 1 is fixed: the PO inherits the HCH org that FT learners built and GT learners ran — undocumented, opinionated, real. Sprints 2–3 move to live client delivery.
Why: "inheriting a mess" costs you nothing to build — your own learners already made it. Ecosystem continuity becomes curriculum.
Option C
Client work + injected hard cases
All 12 weeks are client work; when the client does not produce the week's challenge, the coach injects it — a migration, a bad build, a scope fight.
Cost: heavy coach load, every week, forever.
Take B, and keep C's injection valve. Fixed Sprint 1 on the inherited Horizon org guarantees the floor. In Sprints 2–3 the client sets the work and the week sets the lens — and when a client week genuinely cannot carry the lens, the coach drops in a prepared hard case rather than rescheduling the curriculum. One valve, used rarely, instead of a parallel program.
04 · RESOLVE

The PO column your Cross-Role Alignment Matrix is missing

The handbook's matrix defines four actors — Technical Builder, Business Analyst, Accidental Admin, Client. The Product Owner appears once in the roles document, as a line item: "owns project priorities and business outcomes." That sentence is the whole specification. Here is the column, written the same way the other four are: what the PO does in each phase, and what they hand over.

RRecognize
Refuses to accept a tool request as a requirement. Restates it as a problem with a mission cost attached, and holds the disciplined pause when the client asks for a Flow by name.
DeliverableAutomation Opportunity Snapshot, co-signed with the BA — symptom, root cause, mission risk.
EExplore
Builds a rough version of two or three options in a sandbox so option cost is known rather than guessed. This is the estimation-literacy bar, and it is the PO’s job, not the Builder’s.
DeliverableOption cost read — what each path costs and who maintains it after handover.
SSelect
Owns the trade-off and owns the no. Names what is being given up, in the client’s language, before the charter is signed.
DeliverableCo-Design Admin Decision Log entry + Prioritization Decision Log — the call, the alternative, what it cost.
OOutline
Sequences the work and defines done. The BA writes the INVEST stories and Gherkin criteria; the PO decides the order, names the non-functional requirements, and publishes the not-now list.
DeliverableSequenced backlog with a definition of done the squad agreed to, and an explicit not-now list.
LLaunch
Owns the release decision — deploy window, go or no-go on a failed smoke test, and what the nonprofit hears about it. Adoption is a PO deliverable, not a training afterthought.
DeliverableGo/no-go on the Deployment Playbook + release notes and demo script written for a program director.
VVerify
Decides what a failed UAT means: fix, accept, or defer. Then answers the question the scripts cannot — did this solve the problem named in Recognize, measured against reclaimed capacity, SLA rate, or data integrity.
DeliverableUAT disposition record + value verification against the original problem statement.
EEvolve
Triages the Evolution Log. Decides which requests open a new Recognize cycle and which are deferred with a reason the requester can read.
DeliverableEvolution Log triage — defect, enhancement, training gap, or future phase, each with a justification.
The resolvePhases data in Trail OS is classified authoritative and feeds Penny’s RAG corpus, but it lists Evaluate, Solve, Organize, Leverage and a spurious eighth phase called Execute — against the handbook’s Explore, Select, Outline, Launch. Penny will teach the wrong acronym to a learner who asks. Worth a correction ticket before the PO track is written, since every phase gate in this document references the real seven.
05 · The twelve weeks

Sprint 1 fixed. Sprints 2–3 are lenses on live work. Every week names its RESOLVE phase.

Sprint 1 · The Inherited Org Weeks 1–4 · fixed capstone · Horizon Community Hub, as left by GT
Week 1
Read the org
R · Recognize
You are handed HCH and no documentation. Produce an org assessment: what exists, what it costs to keep, what is load-bearing, what is quietly broken. Deliver a keep / change / retire call on twelve findings — with a reason for each.
Evidence: Org Assessment + first Prioritization Decision Log entries.
Week 2
Build it rough — estimation literacy
E · Explore
Take three requests off the HCH backlog and build a throwaway version of each in your own sandbox. Not production quality. Then write down what you now believe each one costs, and what you would ask a developer that you would not have thought to ask last week.
Self-check with Penny, ungraded. The artifact is the changed estimate, not the build.
Week 3
Config review for POs — the smells
S · Select
A named smell catalogue: a Flow doing twelve things, permissions on the profile instead of a permission set, hardcoded IDs, no error path, validation logic in three places, automation nobody can turn off. Review a deliberately bad HCH build, find the smells, and write how you would raise each one with the person who built it.
Evidence: Config Review Notes — the smell, the risk, the ask.
Week 4
A backlog out of a mess
O · Outline
Turn the assessment into a sequenced, refined backlog Elena would fund: acceptance criteria, non-functional requirements named out loud, a definition of done the squad agrees to, and an explicit not-now list. Defend the sequence to your coach.
Coach gate 1. Then the PO joins a live client squad.
Sprint 2 · Delivery Under Pressure Weeks 5–8 · live client work · the client sets the work, the week sets the lens
Week 5 · lens
Refinement
O · Outline
Whatever your client needs this week, examine it through refinement. One story, before and after, with the questions that changed it.
Week 6 · lens
Saying no, sequencing under uncertainty
S · Select
Something real gets deferred this week. Write the no — to the stakeholder, in their language — and log what it cost.
Week 7 · lens
Data migration as a product problem
E · Explore
Who decides what "good enough data" means, what gets left behind, and who signs off. The migration is never just a technical task.
Week 8 · lens
Non-functional requirements & what done means
O · Outline
Volume, permissions, error paths, who maintains it after you leave. Name them before the build, not in UAT.
Sprint 3 · Ship It and Hand It Over Weeks 9–12 · live client work · release, acceptance, handover, defense
Week 9 · lens
Release management the PO owns
L · Launch
Sandboxes, deploy windows, what a CI/CD pipeline will and will not protect you from. Enough Salesforce DevOps literacy to sequence a release, not to run one.
Week 10 · lens
Acceptance without a QA team
V · Verify
Write and run UAT with nonprofit staff who have day jobs. Decide what a failed test means: fix, accept, or defer.
Week 11 · lens
Demo and release notes for a nonprofit audience
L · Launch
Run the demo. Write the release notes a program director will actually read. Adoption is a PO deliverable.
Week 12 · fixed
Handover and the panel defense
E · Evolve
Hand the backlog to whoever comes next, then defend three decisions to a panel: one prioritization call, one no, one bad approach you caught.
06 · Rhythm

The squad week

Stand-up — timeboxed, taught
Everyone gives their work. Fifteen minutes, held. Not a status meeting. Proper protocol is itself curriculum in Sprint 1 — the PO learns to protect the box, and the coach models it for two weeks before handing it over.
Immediately after: the week's topic and assignments are walked through so nobody starts confused.
Campfire — working session
The squad works a topic together, or somebody demos. The PO runs the demo from Week 6 onward. This is where the lens gets applied to the client's actual work in front of witnesses.
Monthly private coaching, 45 minutes: Week 4 gate, Week 8 mid-course correction, Week 12 defense prep and what comes next.
The squad, as designed — 5 to 7 including the coach
Coach · Business Analyst · Solutions Architect · Product Owner · one to three admins or consultants. Work happens in sandboxes, promoted through Salesforce DevOps.
The one role question to settle now: where does the PO end and the BA begin? If both are learners in the same squad, they will collide in week two. My proposal — the BA owns discovery and process mapping; the PO owns sequence, tradeoff, and acceptance. The BA finds out what is true; the PO decides what happens because of it. Until the squad is full, the coach plays the missing seats and says which hat they are wearing.
07 · Evidence & gates

Grade the reasoning, not the outcome

Whether the thing shipped has too many confounds — a client can stall a perfect PO. So the record is what they decided and how they defended it. Three artifacts carry the program, and they replace the Admin Decision Log for this track.

Prioritization Decision Log
One entry a week minimum. The call, the options, the tradeoff, and what you said no to. At least two entries where you were wrong.
Config Review Notes
The smells you caught before they shipped: what you saw, the risk you named, how you raised it, and what the squad did.
Release Notes & Demo Scripts
Written for a nonprofit audience. Plain language, no jargon, and honest about what did not make this release.
Completion — my recommendation, since you asked me to call it
Reuse the coach gate you already built — advisory, blocking, critical — with PO-specific triggers, and finish with a panel defense. Client sign-off goes in the record as context, never as a grade.
The Estimate Test — the PO track's version of the Elena Test. Can this learner say why a request is expensive, in a way an engineer would agree with? If they can only repeat what they were told, they are relaying, not owning.
The No Test — has this learner deferred something real, in writing, to a stakeholder who wanted it? A PO who has never said no has not been a PO yet. Blocking at the Week 8 gate.
Panel defense, Week 12 — coach plus one person who was not in the squad. Three decisions, fifteen minutes each. The panel is allowed to disagree with the decision and still pass the defense.
Penny — three switchable modes
Sparring partner. Pushes back on weak prioritization reasoning. "You sequenced that first because it was easy. Say the business reason."
Stakeholder simulator. Plays Elena, or a board member who wants the thing you deferred. The PO rehearses the no before delivering it.
Config-smell reviewer. The PO describes the proposed approach; Penny asks what breaks at scale and who maintains it.
Penny never clears a gate and never rehearses a real client conversation on the learner's behalf. Simulator mode is practice; the coach clears client-facing work.
08 · Length

Twelve weeks holds — but only just, and one thing does not fit

The craft fits in twelve. What does not fit is the arc that actually makes a PO credible: earning a stakeholder's trust, spending it on a hard call, and living with the result. That takes two engagements, not one — and no curriculum can compress it.

Do not stretch to sixteen. Add a residency instead.
Keep the 12-week core as the credentialed program with a clean finish. Then offer a rolling PO Residency: the graduate stays on a squad for a second engagement, keeps monthly coaching, drops the weekly assignments, and takes over standing up the next PO. Low coach cost, and it gives you the thing you cannot fake — a PO with two clients behind them and a graduate who can teach.
A 16-week fixed program costs more coach time, raises the price, and thins the cohort. The residency gets you the same depth without touching the core.
09 · Pressure-test these

Five assumptions I am making. Break the wrong ones.

1. The HCH org that GT leaves behind is messy enough — and reachable enough — to be a real inheritance exercise in Week 1.
2. A PO can be dropped into a live client squad at Week 5 without the client feeling like a training ground.
3. Every squad has a real nonprofit stakeholder willing to be told no by a learner — or a coach who will play one convincingly and consistently.
4. The Salesforce Business Analyst exam is close enough to the PO craft that anchoring to it does not distort what you teach.
5. One coach can hold a stand-up, a campfire, a monthly 1:1 per learner, and a client relationship — for a squad of 5 to 7.
