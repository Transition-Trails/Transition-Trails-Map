Transition Trails · Guided Trail

Certified. Now prove it.

Program design for a 12-week cohort of 3 to 5 certified, unhired admins who inherit a working org and take coach-assigned client work alongside it. No direct client contact — that is Trail of Mastery. Nothing here rebuilds what the Foundations Trail built.

01 · The bar

A working org, real consequences, and no script

Entry is the certification
Admin certification passed, not in progress. A Foundations graduate who has not sat the exam waits. This keeps the cohort at one level and lets every week assume the exam concepts are already there.
Real work, shielded interface
The inherited capstone teaches the craft on a safe org. Coach-assigned client and TT work provides the consequences. The coach holds the client relationship and relays the requirement; the learner never speaks to the nonprofit. Direct client contact is Trail of Mastery.
Penny changes role
In Foundations, Penny is the primary coach — the trail can be walked alone toward an exam. In Guided Trail the coach leads and Penny becomes the interviewer: challenging decisions, rehearsing answers, never supplying them.
Two one-hour sessions, a weekly capstone increment, assigned client work, and testing a buddy’s build is a lot for someone job-hunting. The capstone should be the lighter workstream — roughly a third of the week. If a week has to give, it is the capstone that gives, because the assigned work has a real deadline and the capstone does not. Splitting the sessions across the week helps: the stand-up sets the work, the campfire closes it.
02 · The inherited org

Three ways to hand someone a real mess

Every GT learner needs an org that already works and already has problems. Where that org comes from changes what the program can promise.

Option A
A peer's org, as they left it
Each learner inherits a Foundations graduate's actual org. Maximum realism — the mess is authentic, the documentation gaps are real, and nobody planted anything.
Cost: ungradeable. Two learners get wildly different difficulty, and a tidy predecessor leaves nothing to find. Also needs a supply of finished FT orgs you do not yet have.
Option B · recommended
A seeded org TT controls
One master HCH org, built to the Foundations end state, with seven known defects planted and a sealed findings list held by the coach. Every learner gets the same starting difficulty.
Why: gradeable, repeatable, and available before any FT cohort finishes. It is also the starter org section 3.3 of the current blueprint already promises but does not exist.
Option C
A peer's org, then seeded
Take a real FT graduate org and plant the same seven defects into it. Authentic texture, controlled difficulty.
Cost: per-learner setup work every cohort, forever. Right answer eventually; wrong answer for Cohort 1.
Start at B, plan for C. Cohort 1 needs a controlled org you can grade against and hand to someone next week. Once Foundations has produced a few finished orgs, seeding real ones gives you the texture without giving up the grading. Option A never becomes safe on its own.

04b · The squad

Coach assigns, buddy tests, coach releases

Alongside the capstone, each learner takes real work the coach assigns from client and TT backlogs. The learner does not hold the client relationship. Three roles, and nothing reaches a production org without the third.

Cadence: two one-hour sessions a week on separate days — stand-up early in the week to set the work, campfire late to close it. Days are set per cohort once the roster is known. Replays count, to a maximum of two.
The coach assigns
Work arrives as a requirement, not a ticket — the coach relays what the nonprofit needs and holds back the solution. The learner still has to refine it, ask what is missing, and say what it will cost.
The coach is the stakeholder proxy. Deliberately imperfect at explaining what they want — because real ones are.
The buddy tests
Learners pair and test each other’s work. This is the best structural decision in the program: it makes the UAT script criterion literal. "Runnable by someone who did not build it" stops being hypothetical when a peer is about to run it and will say so if it is not.
Being tested teaches as much as testing. Both sides produce a record.
The coach releases
Nothing enters a client production org without coach review. The learner assembles the change set and requests release; the coach approves, defers, or sends it back with a reason.
A deferred release is a teaching moment, not a failure — and it is the closest thing to a real change advisory board a learner will meet before they are hired.
The client brief One per client · generated from the Digital Compass assessment, coach-edited · issued at assignment
This is the standard for how much context a learner gets, so it stops being a per-coach habit. NDAs are signed at onboarding, so the brief can be specific. Generated from the Digital Compass assessment output, then edited by the coach. Five parts:
1 · What they do
Mission, who they serve, staff size, funding model. Enough that a learner can reason about why a requirement matters — a grant deadline explains more than a priority field ever will.
2 · What we know about their org
Edition, installed packages, integrations, who administers it, and the debt already found. Facts only.
3 · What we do not know
Stated explicitly. This is the most useful section — it marks the discovery the learner is expected to do rather than leaving them to guess where the map ends.
4 · How this client is handled
Who at TT owns the relationship, what the release cadence is, and what the client has been told about who does the work.
5 · What is publishable
Anonymised by default — "a 40-user nonprofit org with a grant deadline" — and named only with written client consent. Stated per client at assignment, so a learner never discovers the limit in Week 11.
Anonymised by default, named only with written client consent. The reasoning is what an employer is buying, and it survives anonymisation almost intact — "a nonprofit with a 40-user org and a grant deadline" carries nearly all the interview value of naming them. Two consequences worth building in: consent has to be requested at engagement start rather than at Week 12 when the learner needs it, and the capstone portfolio has to be strong enough to stand alone in case consent never comes.
If two learners are assigned the same requirement they will converge on the same solution and test their own assumptions back at each other. Assign different work and cross-test — the value is in reading a system you did not build, which is the same muscle the inherited capstone trains.
05 · Deliverables

Two every week, six rotating

Every week, without exception
Decision Log entry — continuing from #13, since Foundations produces exactly twelve.
The 90-second answer — this week's work as an interview response. Recorded, not written. Twelve of these by Week 12, and the improvement from the first to the last is itself evidence.
Rotating, where the work calls for it
Current-state process flow · config review note · stakeholder recommendation with options and cost · change plan with a rollback · UAT script someone else can run · release note written for Elena · training guide
Each appears two or three times across twelve weeks. Producing all eight every week would make them homework rather than artifacts.
06 · Support, and the evidence

Replacing the walkthroughs, and proving readiness

Option Cards — during the week
Two or three viable approaches to the week's problem with tradeoffs named, and no steps. One page. Teaches Select from the RESOLVE handbook, which is exactly the muscle an unhired admin has never had to use.
Decision Debriefs — after DoD passes
A short recording of a practitioner reasoning through the same problem: what they considered, rejected, and got wrong. Released only once the learner’s own work is done, so it is a mirror rather than a script — and it doubles as interview modeling.
The RESOLVE module — add-on, taught here
Guided Trail is where RESOLVE gets taught, as an add-on module alongside the twelve weeks. That is the right place for it: the method lands when there is real work to apply it to, and every artifact in the rubric maps to a phase of it.
Trail of Mastery offers the same module as catch-up, since an experienced admin can enter there directly from Explorer’s Trail without having taken it.
Concept explainers — before the week
Short generated videos on how a platform feature works — How Email-to-Case automates support — paired with something to read. Concept, never configuration. Visuals and reading alongside the doing, not instead of it.
These do not break the no-script rule, and the distinction is worth being strict about: an explainer says what the feature is for and where it breaks. A walkthrough says which buttons to press. The first is knowledge a certified admin should already have and often does not; the second is the thing Guided Trail removes.
What a coach never does
Confirm a configuration is correct before the learner has committed to it. Sprint 1 of the current blueprint already says this well — the coach reviews how you present the work, not whether it compiles.
Guided Trail deploys with change sets. That is the right call and not just a simpler one — change sets teach a learner what metadata actually depends on what, because the set fails until they work it out by hand. Source control and CI/CD belong in Trail of Mastery, where the work is client-facing and a bad deploy has a real cost. A learner who has hand-assembled change sets understands what a pipeline is doing for them later.
Evidence for employers — your three, plus four I would add
Confirmed
A portfolio an employer can review without you present · a Demo Day recording usable as a work sample · three defended decisions, with what you rejected
Suggested additions
The findings register. What you inherited, what you flagged, what you left alone and why. Almost no candidate can show this, and it is the single most senior-looking artifact in the set.
A before-and-after measure. One number that moved — queue misroutes, SLA rate, records failing validation. Employers trust a number with a method behind it.
Peer testimony. You worked a real client engagement with two to four others. Their account of how you worked is more credible than a coach's, and free.
The first and twelfth 90-second answer, side by side. Growth is more persuasive than polish, and it is the one thing a bootcamp graduate cannot fake.
07 · Open

Two decided, one still open

1. Someone hired mid-program — decided. A learner who paid may withdraw at any point, though the encouragement is to finish. A scholarship learner completes the program. That asymmetry is defensible and worth stating plainly in enrollment: the scholarship is funded on the expectation of a completed record, and the completed record is what the funder is buying. One practical consequence — a scholarship learner who starts a job in Week 7 will need the load reduced, not the requirement waived, so the capstone increment should be the part that flexes.
2. The client brief — open, own design session. Briefs generate from the Digital Compass assessment output with the coach editing. The one thing to carry into that session: the assessment was built to scope a nonprofit’s needs, not to brief a learner, so it almost certainly does not record what we do not know — the section that makes a brief teach anything rather than just inform.
3. Attendance — decided, and deliberately outside the rubric. Participation is a coach and learner conversation, captured separately from the artifact record. That is the right split: the rubric judges what a learner produced, and mixing in whether they attended live would make the artifact verdicts harder to defend to a funder. Program Engagement still records the sessions, so the data exists without becoming a grade.
